## THE PROBLEM

**A lot of churches are one retirement away from a bookkeeping crisis.**

Many congregations rely on one aging bookkeeper who's been doing it the same way for decades — often on paper, often as an unpaid labor of love. When that person retires or steps back, there's frequently no one trained to take over, and records can fall behind fast. Smaller communities often can't justify hiring a dedicated bookkeeper at all, so volunteers handle it in spare moments, which slows everything down. And when it comes to donor records and financial data, "whoever has time this week" isn't really a system.

## THE MODEL: WHAT STAYS LOCAL, WHAT MOVES TO US

**You don't need a skilled local bookkeeper. You need someone who can deposit money, and someone who can approve spending.**

That's the honest version of what changes. Everything in between — the actual bookkeeping — is on us.

| Locally, you need... | We handle... |
|---|---|
| Someone to count, confirm, and deposit cash and checks | Recording every deposit and matching it to the right fund |
| Someone to approve expenses | Coding, categorizing, and reconciling every transaction |
| — | A U.S.-based finance lead who handles bank access and actually sends payments |

**One safeguard worth spelling out:** our bookkeeping team never has the ability to move money or access your bank account directly. They prepare everything — coded, matched, ready to go — but authorization to actually send a payment always stays with a U.S.-based finance lead. That separation isn't a limitation we're working around; it's how the service is built.

## FAQ

**We collect cash and check donations in person — how does a remote bookkeeper handle that?**
Nothing changes about how your offering is counted and deposited. That stays local, in person — and if your church already has two people count and verify the offering before it's deposited (standard practice at most churches, and a good internal control regardless of who does your books), that continues exactly as it is now. The only new habit is depositing the full amount intact and keeping a simple record of what was deposited, rather than pulling cash out to cover a reimbursement on the spot — that one change is what makes remote reconciliation actually work. Checks are often eligible for mobile deposit through your bank, the same way many churches already do it; cash still needs someone physically local, same as today. Our part starts once the money is deposited: we record and categorize it, reconcile it against your bank statement, match it to donor and pledge records, and make sure it's reflected accurately in your reports. The people you trust to physically handle the offering keep doing exactly that; we handle what happens to the numbers afterward.

**Who actually sees our financial data?**
Sayaka handles the sensitive parts of the work directly. Her supporting team handles routine data entry and transactions under her supervision — access is scoped, not wide open.

**Can your team access our bank account or move money?**
No. The bookkeeping team prepares everything — transactions coded, matched, and ready — but the ability to actually authorize a payment or touch your bank account stays with a U.S.-based finance lead. Nobody overseas has that authority.

**Do we have to switch software?**
No. We start with whatever you're already using. If you'd like to modernize later, that's a separate conversation on your timeline.

**What if our bookkeeper is retiring soon and we're worried about the gap?**
This is one of the most common reasons churches reach out to us. Tell us your timeline and we'll build a transition plan around it.

## What the competitive brief changed in this draft

- **"Meet Sayaka" is a first-class section**, not a footnote — the brief found that neither BELAY nor Jitasa names the actual bookkeeper doing the work; both keep it anonymous ("your dedicated bookkeeper") or size-matched. A real name and a real, honest description of how she works is the one thing this page can do that the two biggest competitors don't.
- **The "we learn your system before we change anything" line is stated as an explicit promise**, not left implicit — this came directly from what Sayaka herself said in the Aug 26 call, and neither competitor makes this kind of concrete process commitment on their page.
- **The transition/succession scenario is named directly** ("your bookkeeper is retiring and there's no one to replace them") rather than described abstractly as "outsourcing benefits" — this is a real pain point PWA has already heard from a prospective client (Mark's own church), and neither comp addresses it head-on.
- **No scale or tenure claims were added**, even though both BELAY and Jitasa lean on exactly that (Jitasa: "75+ churches... since 2008"). PWA doesn't have real numbers like that yet, so the honest move is to lean harder on the named-person differentiator instead of implying a scale that isn't there.
- **Pricing stays quote-based**, consistent with both competitors and with the fact that PWA doesn't have a confirmed standard rate for this service yet — but the page explains why, so it doesn't read as evasive.
- **Added the operating-model detail you supplied on the remote-bookkeeping architecture** (role separation between local deposit/approval and remote bookkeeping, and the money-movement safeguard restricting payment authority to a U.S.-based finance lead). This directly answers the "how can a remote bookkeeper handle our cash?" objection with something more specific and honest than a generic reassurance — and neither BELAY nor Jitasa addresses money-movement authority on their pages at all, so it's a genuine point of difference. Left out: the QuickBooks import mechanics and internal SOP artifacts (deposit sheets, exception queues, payment queues), since those are operationally correct but belong in onboarding documentation, not a landing page, and the QuickBooks import method specifically is still being piloted rather than decided.
